**Bottom line.** In every tested scenario, scheduling the LQR weights on SoH changes energy
per km by −0.0019 % to +0.034 % and never improves tracking. This holds for all three
battery-limit classes. A single fixed gain, retuned once, beats the scheduled controller
everywhere. SoH changes the vehicle's behaviour substantially only when the current limits
are *assumed* to shrink with SoH (class C), and then it changes both controllers equally.

## 3. Results by evidence class

**Conventions:**
- ΔE % = 100·(E_S − E_F)/E_F. **The denominator is the fixed controller F.**
- E is net battery chemical energy ∫V_oc·I dt per *actual* km.
- RMSE ratio = RMSE_S/RMSE_F.
- **Decision rule** (pre-registered): |ΔE| < 1 % means NO PRACTICAL DIFFERENCE.
- "Incomplete" means a distance shortfall above 1 %. Energy per km is then not comparable
  between scenarios, because the vehicle did not complete the cycle.

**What "no practical difference" does and does not mean.**
- It means the differences lie inside the pre-registered ±1 % engineering margin.
- It is not a statistical-equivalence claim.
- The largest observed difference (0.034 %) is about 29 times smaller than that margin. The

  smaller than the ≈ 1 % resolution at which this model, with its constant efficiencies and
  constant V_oc, can rank energy use.

## 4. What causes the SoH-dependent effects

The matched contrasts below use controller F (`results/analysis_mechanism.csv`), with
energy as Wh per actual km. The table shows UDDS at SoH 0.7; the other cycles behave the
same way and are listed in the CSV.

| Pathway | Contrast | ΔE | ΔRMSE | Friction brake | Regenerated energy | Battery-limited time | Shortfall |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| R0/R1 growth only | B0, SoH 0.7 vs 1.0 | +0.351 Wh/km (+0.50 %) | 0 | 0 | −0.46 Wh | 0 | 0 |
| Voltage window | BV vs B0 | **0** | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| 4× faster resistance growth | BV-k2 vs BV | +1.036 Wh/km (+1.47 %) | 0 | 0 | −1.58 Wh | 0 | 0 |
| Fixed current limits | BCN vs BV | +4.581 Wh/km (+6.49 %) | +0.121 m/s (+168 %) | +73.3 Wh | −60.3 Wh | +4.2 % | +0.27 pp |
| **SoH scaling of the limits** | BCS vs BCN | +0.925 Wh/km (+1.23 %) | +0.442 m/s (+228 %) | +52.0 Wh | −43.2 Wh | +7.3 % | +1.58 pp |
| Stricter C-rates | BCS2 vs BCS | +1.804 Wh/km (+2.37 %) | +0.846 m/s (+133 %) | +90.6 Wh | −79.7 Wh | +13.4 % | +4.07 pp |
| **Gain scheduling** | S vs F, BCS | +0.0175 Wh/km (+0.023 %) | +0.0003 m/s (+0.05 %) | +0.15 Wh | −0.003 Wh | +0.01 % | +0.0006 pp |
| Retuned fixed gain | F01 vs F, BCS | −0.144 Wh/km (−0.19 %) | −0.0032 m/s (−0.51 %) | −1.07 Wh | +0.28 Wh | −0.08 % | −0.0065 pp |

**Mechanism by pathway:**

1. **Real change in plant dynamics.**
   - None in classes A and B: SoH does not enter the vehicle dynamics, and the voltage window
     never binds.
   - In class C, SoH changes the available drive and regenerative torque. That is the only
     route by which SoH alters the trajectory.
2. **Reduced usable capacity.**
   - In this model, capacity enters only the SoC rate and, in class C, the current limits.
   - The OCV is constant and no SoC limit is reached, so reduced capacity has **no** effect
     except through the assumed C-rate law.
3. **Increased internal resistance.**
   - This raises I²R losses and therefore energy (+0.17 % to +0.97 % at SoH 0.7 with
     k_r0 = 0.5; up to +2.8 % on US06 with k_r0 = 2.0).
   - It does not affect tracking.
   - It affects F and S identically, because neither controller can avoid the losses
     without tracking differently.
4. **Discharge-current limit.**
   - It is the dominant cause of the RMSE increase and of the distance shortfall.
5. **Charge-current (regeneration) limit.**
   - It is the dominant cause of the energy increase on S1 and UDDS. Regeneration is cut
     and the excess goes to the friction brake: +52 Wh on UDDS from SoH scaling alone.
   - On US06 energy per km *falls* with SoH (−15 % for BCS vs BCN). That is an artefact of
     not completing the cycle (+10 pp shortfall), not an efficiency gain.
6. **Gain scheduling.**
   - It changes energy by at most 0.034 % and never reduces RMSE.
   - The thesis's law lowers the torque-state weight and raises R as SoH falls, so control
     becomes slightly less aggressive.
   - When the battery limits bind, both controllers sit on the same limit, so their
     trajectories nearly coincide (`figures/udds_bcs_soh07_timeseries.png`).
7. **Tuning asymmetry.**
   - F and S share the same pre-registered Bryson base and the same anti-windup, limits and
     plant.
   - F01 was selected once on S1 in the earlier study (S-C family) and is out-of-sample
     elsewhere. Its advantage over S shows that a better *fixed* tuning matters more than
     SoH scheduling.
   - S's γ = 1 is an assumption. In the earlier S-B sweep, γ from 0 to 10 changed energy by
     at most 0.04 % on S1/B0.
8. **Numerical artefact.**
   - Excluded: the sanity controls are exact, and the differences are 1e5–1e8 times larger
     than the timestep-convergence change.

## 5. Sensitivity to the assumed current-limit law

| Assumption varied | Effect on behaviour (controller F, SoH 0.7) | Effect on the S vs F comparison |
|---|---|---|
| Limits scaled with SoH (BCS) vs not (BCN) | Large on UDDS and US06 (RMSE +228 % and +70 %); none on S2 (the limits never bind); small on S1 (+8.9 % RMSE) | None: ΔE stays within 0–0.035 % in every case |
| 3 C / 1 C vs 2 C / 0.5 C | Stricter limits bind even at SoH 1.0 (UDDS RMSE 0.72 m/s) and dominate behaviour | None |
| k_r0 0.5 vs 2.0 | Energy rises by 0.5–2.8 %; the current limits bind slightly earlier | None |
| Voltage window on or off | No effect at any tested resistance (k_r0 ≤ 2.0) | None |

**Conclusion.** The *existence* of an SoH effect on driving behaviour depends entirely on the
assumed law I_max ∝ SoH. The *absence* of a benefit from SoH scheduling does not depend on
any tested assumption.

## 7. Limitations

**Model and parameters:**
- One hypothetical vehicle, with parameters taken from the thesis without primary sources.
- Constant motor/inverter efficiency.
- Constant OCV.
- Flat road.
- No thermal model.
- SoH is known exactly (the best case for scheduling).
- Gains are designed at 30 km/h and also used on highway-speed segments.

**Battery limits:**
- The window and C-rates are assumptions, not datasheet values. The cell is unknown (owner
  item OC-1).
- The torque lag causes measurable current-limit exceedance, up to 50 A·s.
- No implementation of the battery window independent of this one exists.

**Experimental coverage:**
- US06 exceeds the vehicle's capability in every class. UDDS does so under the stricter
  class-C limits.
- Energy per km in those runs is not comparable across scenarios. It is reported, but no
  conclusion rests on it.
- Only the thesis's scheduling law (γ_v = γ_u = 1) was tested here. A controller designed
  to *use* knowledge of the reduced limits was not tested. That would be a new design, not
  the thesis's claim, and needs its own protocol amendment and output directory.

**Validation:**
- Nothing here is validated against a vehicle or a battery.
- No independent human replication has been performed.

## 8. Strongest conclusion supported by the current evidence

In a documented, numerically verified longitudinal EV model, the SoH-scheduled LQR weight
law from the thesis gave no practical benefit over a fixed-gain LQR. It changed energy per
km by at most ±0.034 % and never improved tracking:
- with the original plant;
- with a battery voltage window and fixed current limits;
- with current limits *assumed* to shrink in proportion to SoH.

A single fixed gain, retuned once, outperformed it in every tested combination.

Battery ageing does alter the vehicle's behaviour when the battery's current limits fall with
SoH: tracking degrades, regeneration is lost and cycle distance falls short. But it alters
both controllers alike, and weight scheduling does not compensate.

Two claims in the thesis are not supported here:
- the 12.5 % energy claim is contradicted within this model;
- the 15.3 % fade claim cannot be assessed, because no credible fade model exists.
